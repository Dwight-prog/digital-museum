Symbols Through History

How national symbols became connected to important moments in Philippine history. Select a card to expand and learn more.

1898
🇵🇭

Event 01

Philippine Independence

The Philippines declares independence in Kawit, Cavite.

On June 12, 1898, General Emilio Aguinaldo proclaimed Philippine independence from Spanish colonial rule during a ceremony in Kawit, Cavite. The Philippine flag, which had been sewn by Marcela Mariño Agoncillo with the help of her daughter Lorenza and Delfina Herbosa Natividad, was officially unfurled for the first time in public.

The declaration marked the culmination of years of revolutionary struggle, beginning with the Katipunan uprising in 1896. It also signaled the birth of Asia’s first republic, though the new nation would soon face a new colonial power — the United States.

Connection to Symbols

The flag’s eight-rayed sun and three stars were born from this exact moment. The sun’s rays represented the first eight provinces that rose against Spanish rule — Manila, Cavite, Bulacan, Pampanga, Nueva Ecija, Tarlac, Laguna, and Batangas.

Connection to Identity

This day marked the first time Filipinos could claim a shared national identity, expressed through a flag of their own design and a declaration read before a gathered crowd.

1899
🏛️

Event 02

First Philippine Republic

The Malolos Republic is established — Asia’s first constitutional republic.

On January 23, 1899, the First Philippine Republic was inaugurated in Malolos, Bulacan, with Emilio Aguinaldo as its president. The Malolos Constitution, drafted by a revolutionary congress, established a republican government with three branches — executive, legislative, and judicial — making it the first constitutional republic in Asia.

The republic was short-lived. Conflict with American forces had already begun in February 1899, and by 1901, Aguinaldo was captured. The republic was eventually dissolved, but its founding principles would inspire future generations.

Connection to Symbols

The Malolos Republic adopted the same flag and symbols of the 1898 declaration, giving them formal state recognition for the first time. The republic’s seal and official documents bore the sun, stars, and colors that would later become permanent national emblems.

Connection to Identity

The establishment of a functioning Filipino government — however brief — proved that Filipinos could govern themselves. It planted the idea of sovereignty that would endure through decades of colonial rule.

1934
🌸

Event 03

National Flower & Tree

Sampaguita and Narra are formally declared national symbols.

On February 1, 1934, American Governor-General Frank Murphy issued Proclamation No. 652, declaring the sampaguita (Jasminum sambac) as the national flower and the narra (Pterocarpus vidalianus) as the national tree. The proclamation cited the sampaguita’s popularity, ornamental value, fragrance, and its role in Filipino legends and traditions, while the narra was chosen for its hardiness, utility, and historical significance.

The declaration came during a period of growing Filipino self-awareness under American colonial rule, just a year before the Commonwealth was established.

Connection to Symbols

By choosing a small, fragrant flower and a sturdy hardwood tree, the proclamation anchored nature itself into the national identity. The sampaguita represented simplicity and grace; the narra, strength and resilience.

Connection to Identity

These symbols were familiar to ordinary Filipinos — the sampaguita sold on street corners, the narra growing in plazas and forests. Their formal recognition connected everyday life to the emerging idea of a Filipino nation.

1935
📜

Event 04

Commonwealth Era

A transitional government prepares the nation for full sovereignty.

The Commonwealth of the Philippines was inaugurated on November 15, 1935, with Manuel L. Quezon as president and Sergio Osmeña as vice president. It was established as a ten-year transitional government under the Tydings-McDuffie Act, preparing the country for full independence from the United States.

The Commonwealth had its own constitution, elected legislature, and control over domestic affairs, though foreign policy and defense remained under American supervision. The period saw significant efforts to build national institutions and strengthen Filipino identity.

Connection to Symbols

The Commonwealth government adopted official seals and heraldic symbols that incorporated elements from the Philippine flag and earlier revolutionary emblems. The sampaguita and narra, declared national symbols just a year earlier, became part of this growing visual identity.

Connection to Identity

For the first time, Filipinos were governing themselves under their own constitution. The Commonwealth represented a bridge between colonial status and full independence, and it fostered a sense of nationhood that would carry through the war years ahead.

1946
🕊️

Event 05

Independence Recognized

The Philippines is formally recognized as a sovereign republic.

On July 4, 1946, the United States formally recognized Philippine independence, and the Republic of the Philippines was established with Manuel Roxas as its first president. The date was deliberately chosen to coincide with American Independence Day, though the Philippines would later shift its official Independence Day celebration to June 12.

Independence came after World War II and the Japanese occupation (1942–1945), during which Filipino and American forces fought to liberate the islands. The war left the country devastated, but also solidified a strong sense of national identity and pride.

Connection to Symbols

The flag, once flown in resistance and revolution, now flew over a fully sovereign state. The national anthem was sung not as a song of protest, but as the official anthem of a recognized nation. The symbols that had been born in struggle were now the everyday emblems of a free country.

Connection to Identity

Filipinos could now shape their own destiny as a nation. The symbols that had represented hope during colonial rule became expressions of a people’s pride and responsibility for their own future.

1987
⚖️

Event 06

1987 Constitution

A new constitution enshrines the nation’s symbols in law.

Following the People Power Revolution of 1986, a constitutional commission was convened to draft a new constitution. The 1987 Constitution was ratified by the Filipino people on February 2, 1987, and it remains the supreme law of the Philippines today.

The new constitution restored democratic institutions after years of authoritarian rule and reaffirmed the values of sovereignty, human rights, and national identity. It also provided legal protection for the nation’s symbols, including the flag, anthem, and other emblems.

Connection to Symbols

The 1987 Constitution formally defined the national flag and anthem, and it mandated respect for these symbols. Republic Act No. 8491, the Flag and Heraldic Code, would later specify the exact rules for displaying, raising, and honoring them.

Connection to Identity

After years of dictatorship, the constitution reasserted that national symbols belonged to the people — not to any single leader or regime. Respect for the flag and anthem became a civic duty tied to democratic ideals.

1995
🦅

Event 07

Philippine Eagle

The Philippine Eagle is declared the national bird.

On July 4, 1995, President Fidel V. Ramos issued Proclamation No. 615, declaring the Philippine Eagle (Pithecophaga jefferyi) as the national bird of the Philippines. The proclamation cited the eagle’s uniqueness, strength, power, and love for freedom as qualities that “exemplify the Filipino people.”

The Philippine Eagle, also known as the monkey-eating eagle, is one of the largest and rarest eagles in the world, found only in the forests of Luzon, Samar, Leyte, and Mindanao. It replaced the maya, a small weaver bird, as the national bird.

Connection to Symbols

The declaration gave the Philippines a national bird that was truly its own — a species found nowhere else on Earth. It also turned the eagle into a powerful symbol of wildlife conservation, drawing attention to the country’s vanishing forests.

Connection to Identity

The eagle’s strength and rarity resonated with Filipino pride. But its endangered status also became a reminder that national symbols can carry a responsibility — in this case, the duty to protect the country’s natural heritage for future generations.

2009
🥋

Event 08

Arnis

Arnis is recognized as the national martial art and sport.

On December 11, 2009, President Gloria Macapagal-Arroyo signed Republic Act No. 9850, formally declaring Arnis as the national martial art and sport of the Philippines. The law defines Arnis as an indigenous Filipino martial art characterized by swinging and twirling movements, along with striking, thrusting, and parrying techniques for defense and offense.

Arnis is also known by other names in different regions, including Eskrima, Kali, Garrote, Pananandata, Pagkalikali, Kabaraon, and Didja. The law directed the Philippine Sports Commission to inscribe the symbol of Arnis in its official seal and to make it the first sport played in the annual Palarong Pambansa.

Connection to Symbols

Arnis became the latest addition to the roster of officially recognized national symbols. Unlike the flag or the sampaguita, it represents a living practice — a martial art that continues to be taught, practiced, and performed.

Connection to Identity

Recognizing Arnis honored the indigenous fighting systems developed long before colonial rule. It connected modern Filipinos to the skills and traditions of their ancestors, and it affirmed that Filipino identity includes physical discipline and cultural heritage.
